Why MIA exists

Memories are more than files.

Photographs can survive while the story around them disappears. Voices fade. Names get forgotten. Places lose their meaning. MIA exists to preserve more of the human story.

The human problem

We keep thousands of photographs. We lose the reason they mattered.

A camera roll can tell you what something looked like. It rarely tells you who was there, what was happening, what somebody sounded like, why the moment mattered or what you hoped another person would remember.

MIA began from a simple idea: the story around a memory deserves to survive with the memory itself.

That means photographs, video, voice, people, places, dates and stories belonging together — without pretending technology knows more than the people who lived them.

Where MIA really began

My daughter. My missing piece.

MIA is personal to me. It began with my daughter, and with a simple fear that time could pass and important memories could disappear before I ever had the chance to share them with her.

I wanted somewhere to preserve the photographs, stories, ordinary moments, thoughts and memories I carried for her — so that one day she could look back and know something that mattered deeply to me:

her daddy always loved her, always liked who she was, and was always incredibly proud of her.

That became the starting point for something much bigger. Because every family has things they do not want to lose, and sometimes we only realise how precious those memories are when time, distance, illness or life itself begins to change.

The reason behind MIA

Preserve it while you still can.

For your child. For your partner. For your parents. For your grandchildren. For the person who may need to hear your voice years from now.

MIA is being built to hold those memories safely until the moment they matter most.

Preserve the memory. Preserve the original.

Human at the front. Evidence behind it.

MIA should feel like a beautiful family archive, not a database. But behind the human experience, the original source should remain protected and understandable.

Where information is supplied, supported, inferred or unknown, MIA should say so. It should never quietly turn a suggestion into a fact.

Why ORVIA built it this way

Technology should help preserve the human — not replace them.

Source before synthesis

If a person did not say something, MIA should not pretend they did.

No fabricated memories

AI may help organise, search or transcribe approved material. It must not invent family history or silently rewrite somebody's story.

Human control

The person creating the archive remains in control of what is preserved, shared and prepared for later.

Who MIA is for

Not one kind of family. Not one stage of life.

The same need appears in many different lives: people want the people they love to remember more than a collection of disconnected files. Sometimes that means preserving family history. Sometimes it means recording your voice while you can. Sometimes it means revisiting familiar moments as memory changes. And sometimes it means leaving something meaningful behind for the people you love after you die.
